This distinction was grateful to him though the salary is but 40l. a year. He now turned his thoughts to publication and continued to reside in London, where in 1795, he married a young lady, who died of a dectrine in the course of two years. In 1805, soon after the establishment of the London Institution, he was appointed principal librarian and occupied apartments in the house of his friend Mr. Porson, from the Morning Chronicle; where it is well known to have been inserted upon the best authority. We are there informed that Mr. Porson in early life suffered from disorders which made great inroads on his constitution. There is also an attempt, no doubt dictated by affection, but certainly a vain attempt, to disguise those lamentable habits of intemperance, into which the Professor had unhappily fallen for many years, and which are too notorious to be disputed while “de mortuis nil nisi verum” shall be regarded as a just sentiment. These habits must have prevented his lectures at Cambridge, had there been no other impediment. They also rendered his connexion with the London Institution, otherwise so honourable to that establishment, a measure of doubtful precedence. Nor can the literary world sufficiently regret the noble objects which such a mind might have attained, had his self-command been such as many scholars have discovered. There is a portrait of the late Professor in the possession of his friend Dr. Raine. His library, enriched by his notes, it is expected will be purchased by his College. For an account of his publications and his literary character we refer to a Memoir in the form part of the present number, for which we are indebted to a friend whose own attainments have well qualified him to appreciate those of the learned Professor.

R. C.
